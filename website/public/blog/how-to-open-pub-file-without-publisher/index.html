How to Open a .pub File Without Publisher: 6 Options Compared

PubConv · 26 October 2026 · 8 min read

You've got a .pub file and no Publisher. Maybe your Microsoft 365 plan dropped it, maybe you're on a new PC, or maybe someone sent you a flyer. Here are the realistic options, what they cost, and where each one falls short.

1. Publisher itself

If any PC in your organisation still has a one-time-purchase Office with Publisher, it's the gold standard: open the file and choose File → Save As → PDF. For many files, Microsoft provides a sample PowerShell script that automates Publisher to export folders to PDF. The catch is that it needs Publisher installed, and Microsoft 365 subscribers lost Publisher in October 2026.

2. LibreOffice Draw (free)

LibreOffice is a free, open-source office suite whose Draw program can open .pub files. It's a genuinely useful tool, and for simple documents (text, pictures, shapes, tables) the results are often good. In our tests it struggled with two things that Publisher documents use all the time:

  • WordArt: headlines either vanished or became solid black curved shapes.
  • Linked text boxes: in newsletters where an article continues from one box to the next, the whole article appeared in every box and ran off the page.

3. Online converters

Upload a file, download a PDF. That's handy for a one-off flyer. Before uploading anything containing personal information (pupil names, member lists, addresses), check where the service is based, how long it keeps files, and whether your organisation's data-protection rules allow it. Many online converters rely on the same open-source .pub reader as LibreOffice, so expect similar results on WordArt and newsletters. We didn't test online services for this article.

4. Paid desktop converters

Several Windows programs convert .pub files offline, typically for $29 to $39. We tested the trial of one popular converter on the same sample files. It was fast, but WordArt headlines came out flat, in the wrong font, and cut off ("SUMMER FAI"). Narrow newsletter columns split words mid-word, and some pictures were resized or shifted. Results will differ between products, so test on your own files.

5. Rebuild in Word or PowerPoint

Microsoft suggests Word for newsletters and letterheads, and PowerPoint for posters and signs. That's sensible for templates you'll keep using, but too slow for an archive of hundreds of old files.

6. PubConv

We built PubConv specifically for the two problems above. It reads WordArt directly from the file and draws it with the right font, warp and colour. It also flows newsletter text through linked boxes using Publisher's own rules, so line breaks and hyphens match. It converts whole folders offline, and there's a free trial that works at full quality.

Which should you choose?

  • A few simple files: LibreOffice is free and fine.
  • You still have Publisher: use it while you can.
  • Hundreds of files, private content, WordArt or newsletters: use an offline converter, and test it on your trickiest files first.
